I

The spectrum and penetration depth

How far each wave gets in

Microwaves and infrared are both light, but their wavelengths differ ten-thousandfold, and that decides where the energy lands. A microwave’s field reaches centimetres into wet food before half its power is gone. Infrared is swallowed in the outermost fraction of a millimetre.

Neither carries enough energy per photon to break a chemical bond. All they can do is make molecules move, and moving molecules are heat.

II

Dipolar rotation and Debye relaxation

Water molecules dancing to a field

A water molecule is lopsided: its oxygen hogs electrons, so one end is slightly negative and the other slightly positive. Put it in an electric field that flips 2.45 billion times a second and it tries to turn and follow.

It can’t keep up perfectly. Hydrogen bonds to its neighbours drag on it, so its rotation lags the field. That lag is friction, and friction is heat. Too slow a field and the molecules follow easily; too fast and they barely move. Loss peaks in between, and Debye’s equation predicts where.

ε* = ε∞ + (εs − ε∞) / (1 + jωτ)
ε″ = (εs − ε∞) ωτ / (1 + ω²τ²)

III

Ionic conduction

Salt joins the dance

Dissolved salt splits into sodium and chloride ions. The field shoves them back and forth in opposite directions, and every collision with a water molecule turns their motion into heat. This is a second, separate heating mechanism.

Ionic loss grows as the frequency falls, so salt matters more at 915 MHz. It also has a cost: the more a food absorbs, the shallower the waves reach. That’s why the salty edges of ham overheat while the middle stays cold.

IV

Infrared: molecular vibration and Planck emission

Light that rings a molecule like a bell

Infrared doesn’t turn molecules; it shakes them. Each bond vibrates at its own natural frequencies, and a photon is absorbed only when its energy matches one of them. Water stretches at about 2.9 µm and bends at about 6.1 µm, so radiation near those wavelengths is gobbled up almost instantly.

Hot emitters glow across a broad spectrum set by their temperature. Cooler ceramic heaters peak in the far infrared, right on water’s strongest bands, so they heat only the very surface. Hotter halogen lamps shift energy to shorter wavelengths that slip a few millimetres deeper.

V

Heating through the layers

Inside out, or outside in

Give two identical slabs of potato the same total power, one by microwave and one by infrared. Infrared deposits everything at the surface; the inside waits for slow conduction. Microwaves deposit heat through the depth, while moisture evaporating from the surface keeps the outermost layer cooler than just beneath it.

Run it and watch the infrared slab’s surface dry out and climb past 100 °C. That’s a crust, browning and crisping. The microwave slab cooks faster but stays pale and soft, which is exactly why combination ovens exist.

VI

Standing waves in the oven

Why your oven has a turntable

Microwaves bounce between the metal walls of the oven and interfere with themselves. Where crests meet crests the field is strong; where they cancel, almost nothing happens. These hot spots sit about half a wavelength apart: 6.1 cm at 2.45 GHz.

Lay a plate of cheese in the oven with the turntable off and you’ll melt a pattern of spots. Switch it on and each piece of cheese travels through hot and cold regions, so the heating evens out into rings.
