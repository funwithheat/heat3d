Cold Lab

An atlas of food preservation without heat, drawn at the scale of molecules.Four live plates. In each one, every molecule carries its own temperature, so you can watch energy arrive, spread and fade, and see how little of it ever becomes heat.

I

High pressure processing

Six thousand atmospheres, and the vitamins don’t notice

Plate I. Isostatic compression of a vegetative cell in water. Insets: Maxwell–Boltzmann speed distribution (bars, simulated; line, theory) and log survival over hold time. Drag sideways across the chamber to set the pressure; hover to probe the local temperature.

Pressure arrives everywhere at once, so a strawberry and the microbe on it are squeezed equally without being crushed. What gives way are the weak, non-covalent bonds that hold membranes and folded proteins in shape.

Vitamins, pigments and flavour molecules are held together by covalent bonds, far too strong for pressure alone. The only heat is a gentle warming from compression, about 3 °C for every 100 MPa, visible as the speed histogram drifts right.

II

Pulsed electric fields

Lightning in microseconds, written on a membrane

Plate II. Electroporation of a 1 µm cell in orange juice. Membrane heads are coloured by local voltage. Insets: oscilloscope trace and transmembrane voltage against angle, with the 1 V threshold. Drag either electrode to change the gap; click the stage to fire a pulse; hover the membrane to read its local voltage.

Each pulse lasts about two millionths of a second. Ions rush toward the electrodes and pile up against the membrane, and the voltage across it follows Schwan’s equation: strongest at the two poles facing the plates, zero at the equator.

Past roughly one volt, the lipid layer tears open. The ions crashing through the juice along the way turn some of the pulse into heat, which is why industrial PEF lines need cooling between treatment chambers.

III

Power ultrasound

Stars the size of a bacterium, lasting a millionth of a second

Plate III. Transient cavitation in juice. Each molecule is coloured by its own temperature. Insets: speed distribution showing the hot tail after each collapse, and the radius history of a single bubble. Click a bubble to collapse it, click empty liquid to seed a new one, and hover to read the local temperature.

Sound stretches the liquid until tiny bubbles open, then squeezes them shut. Over many cycles they grow, until one compression crushes a bubble faster than its gas can escape.

For an instant, the gas inside reaches around 5,000 K. Watch the molecules nearest a collapse flare white, scatter, and hand their energy on to their neighbours. The heat spreads and vanishes within a few molecular diameters, which is why the juice as a whole barely warms.

IV

Ultraviolet C

A photon at 254 nm, and two letters fused for good

Plate IV. Cyclobutane thymine dimers forming in bacterial DNA. Bases: A cyan, T violet, G green, C amber. Insets: DNA absorbance spectrum with the 254 nm mercury line, and survival against dose. Move across the stage to aim the lamp and click to fire a burst of photons.

DNA absorbs ultraviolet most strongly near 260 nm, and a low-pressure mercury lamp emits almost all its light at 254 nm. The match is nearly perfect.

When a photon lands on two thymines sitting side by side, their rings weld together. The kinked strand can no longer be copied, so the cell cannot divide. No heat is involved; the energy goes straight into a chemical bond.
