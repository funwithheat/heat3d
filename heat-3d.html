I

Modes of heat transfer

Three roads into a loaf of bread

Heat content is how much energy something holds; heat transfer is how fast it moves. An oven delivers it three ways at once. Glowing elements radiate infrared straight onto the crust. Hot air loops around and hands over energy by convection. Inside the dough, heat creeps inward molecule to molecule by conduction.

Raise the oven temperature and watch radiation take over: it grows with the fourth power of absolute temperature.

II

Conduction in walls

Fourier’s law, one wall at a time

In steady conduction the temperature falls in a straight line from the hot face to the cold one, and the heat flow is set by three things: how conductive the material is, how big the temperature difference is, and how thick the wall is.

Swap aluminium for foam and the same wall passes eight thousand times less heat. Double the thickness and the flow halves.

q = kA (T1 − T2) / Δx
R = Δx / kA

III

Conduction in multilayered walls and pipes

Resistances in series

A cold-store wall is a sandwich: an air film outside, a steel skin, a thick core of insulation, an aluminium liner, and another air film inside. Heat must cross every layer in turn, so their thermal resistances simply add, like resistors in a circuit.

The temperature drops most where the resistance is largest. On a pipe the layers are rings, and the resistance of each ring depends on the logarithm of its radii.

q = ΔToverall / ΣR
Rring = ln(ro/ri) / 2πkL

IV

Forced and free convection

The thin layer that decides everything

A fluid sliding past a surface sticks to it. Close to the wall a boundary layer forms, slow and insulating, and nearly all the temperature change happens inside it. A thinner boundary layer means a larger heat transfer coefficient, h.

Blow harder and the layer thins: that’s forced convection, measured by the Reynolds number. Switch the fan off and the warmed fluid rises on its own buoyancy: free convection, measured by the Rayleigh number.

V

Overall heat transfer coefficient

Adding up the obstacles

Steam condenses on a tube; milk flows inside. Between them heat meets a condensate film, the metal wall, perhaps a layer of baked-on milk protein, and the milk’s own boundary layer. One number, U, sums them all.

The biggest resistance dominates. Copper barely helps when the milk film is the bottleneck, but a thin layer of fouling can quietly halve the whole exchanger.

1/U = 1/ho + Δx/k + Rf + 1/hi

VI

Heat exchangers

Two streams, one wall

Hot water warms cold milk through a wall. In parallel flow both enter at the same end, the temperature gap starts huge and collapses, and the milk can never get hotter than the water leaves. In counterflow they pass each other, the gap stays more even along the length, and the milk can leave hotter than the water’s exit.

The driving force varies along the exchanger, so designers average it logarithmically.

ΔTlm = (ΔT1 − ΔT2) / ln(ΔT1/ΔT2)
q = UA ΔTlm

VII

Unsteady heating and Heisler charts

Waiting for the centre

Drop a cold potato into boiling water and its temperature changes with both position and time. Two numbers tell the whole story. The Biot number compares resistance inside the food with resistance at its surface; the Fourier number is dimensionless time.

When Bi is large, the surface jumps straight to the water temperature and the centre lags far behind. When Bi is below about 0.1, the food heats almost uniformly. Heisler’s charts plot exactly this; the one below is drawn live from the exact series solution.
